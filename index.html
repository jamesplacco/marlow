The problem

Your student reads fluently, earns A's in English, and scores a 640.

"The passage was fine. I just picked the answer that sounded right."

That sentence is the whole problem. By 9th grade most strong students decode perfectly. What they have never been taught is what to do when a sentence contains a qualifier, when a paragraph turns against itself, or when a writer reports someone else's view without endorsing it. They choose plausible answers without checking the evidence, and the score stays put no matter how many practice tests they take.

Test-prep classes drill question types. Generic tutors reteach grammar. Neither names the specific reasoning error, so neither fixes it. The Marlow Method does one thing on every passage: find the sentence, ask how far it goes, and write down why.

Is this test prep?

It is reading instruction measured with tests. The skills are the ones SAT Reading and Writing, ACT Reading, AP Language, and college seminars all reward, so the score moves. We do not teach tricks, and we do not drill question types for their own sake.

What if my student has a reading difficulty?

The Starter Kit diagnostic includes a short read-aloud fluency screen. If it suggests an unresolved decoding or fluency need, we say so and refer you to a specialist rather than enrolling. This method does not fix those needs, and enrolling those students would misrepresent our results.

The Marlow Method

Find the sentence. Ask how far it goes. Write down why.

Three moves, four skills, eight named errors, one 20-minute routine. The method is what every tutor teaches and what the workspace coaches, so a family gets the same session from any of us.

Reading, from first principles

Reading is two skills multiplied together. We make the first one automatic and teach the second one.

Reading comprehension
= Decoding × Language comprehension

A zero in either term gives zero. That is the whole diagnostic frame.

Writing is a code for speech, not for meaning. The brain has no native reading circuit; it wires a visual word-recognition area to the spoken-language system it already has. You can read a word only as well as you would understand it said aloud.

Decoding becomes automatic; then it stops mattering. After a word has been sounded out a handful of times it is stored as an instant unit. Skilled readers recognize tens of thousands of words without effort, which frees working memory for meaning. By grade 9, nearly every strong student is here.

Past that point, comprehension is mostly knowledge. Vocabulary, background knowledge of the topic, parsing syntax, and inference. A weak reader who knows the subject out-comprehends a strong reader who does not. Strategies help for a few weeks and then saturate; knowledge never does.

The eye caps real reading near 300 to 400 words a minute. Anything faster is skimming, which is a real skill and a different one. We teach it as test craft, never as reading.

Strong listening, weak oral reading

A decoding problem. We do not fix it, and we will not pretend to. The intake screen catches it and refers you to a structured-literacy specialist; the kit fee is refunded.

Strong oral reading, weak listening

A language and knowledge problem. This is our student. The fix is vocabulary by root, knowledge in coherent domains, syntax unpacked, inference checked against the line.

Both weak

Decoding first, because it unlocks the volume that builds everything else. A referral, with a note to come back when oral reading is automatic.

The foundation: automaticity, measured and built

We do not teach phonics. We do build and measure automaticity, because comprehension instruction is wasted on a reader whose attention is still going to the words. Every student takes a one-minute oral reading checkpoint every week: words per minute, accuracy, and phrasing on a passage never used in lessons. Every lesson opens with a timed read-aloud of its first paragraph.

Readers who are accurate but slow get ten minutes of every session on the three drills that move speed: repeated reading of the same paragraph, phrase-cued reading for prosody, and multisyllabic word attack for the long academic words that stall older readers. Readers below 90% accuracy are referred; the text is too hard or decoding is not automatic, and practicing errors makes it worse.

Four skills

What the tests reward and schools under-teach.

Sentence syntax

Untangle long sentences into subject, verb, and the clauses that qualify them. Most missed questions hide in a dependent clause. The workspace has a sentence unpacker that shows what each qualifier does.

Evidence and inference

Point to the line that supports an answer, then say how far it reaches. A plausible answer with no line behind it is a guess, and a correct answer with the wrong line is a guess that got lucky.

Vocabulary in context

Words learned from the passages read that week, grouped by root, distinguished from their near neighbors, used in a sentence, and retrieved on a 1, 3, 7, 14 day schedule.

Evidence-based writing

Short claims with quoted support, revised against feedback that names the exact reasoning error. One or two improvements at a time. Every draft is kept so progress is visible.

Eight named errors

The diagnostic names the pattern. The lessons fix it.

Five modes, one method

Read it, hear it, see it, say it, do it.

Read

Passage left, tasks right, as the test presents it.

Hear

Every passage and word can be read aloud with the sentence highlighted. Qualifiers are easier to hear than to see.

See

Qualifier maps show where the small words sit. Argument maps show claim, evidence, warrant, and limit as boxes.

Say

Forty-five seconds to explain the passage aloud, then a self-rating. If you cannot say the argument, you do not have it.

Do

Match words to meanings, order claims by strength, flip the card before you look.

The mode is the entry point, not the lesson. Students who rotate modes retain more than students who repeat one; the review notebook tracks which modes you use. Set a default on the Resources page →

The software coaches. It never answers, and a human can overrule it.

Three guardrails. The coach never supplies an answer, only the name of the error and a hint. Every piece of feedback can be flagged by the student with one click and lands in the tutor's queue for a decision (uphold, credit, discuss). Any content the model drafts is reviewed by a person before a student sees it; the model is a reviewer and drafter, never an author of record.

Why rules, not just a model. The coach's first version is a reviewed library of rules, not a prompt, so that it cannot hallucinate feedback to a 15-year-old. The rules are the hint sequences and error taxonomy a model is later trained to apply, and the flagged-feedback queue is the dataset that says whether it is right.

4 · Measured

Every decision about a student, a tutor, or the business is made on a number that was logged, not recalled.

Logged, not remembered

Every answer, selected sentence, hint, confidence rating, read-aloud, and closed-book summary is logged with a timestamp. The Friday report is generated from the log and reviewed by the tutor; it is never written from memory.

Independent versus assisted

A right answer after three hints is a different fact from a right answer cold. The two are never averaged. The gap between them is the number that says whether the skill has transferred.

Blind second scoring

A second educator scores a sample of written work from each cohort without knowing which came first. Outcomes are published only after that agreement is recorded.

Gates, not opinions

The business opens a new lane, hires a tutor, or spends on ads only when a stated number is met. The same discipline that governs a student's plan governs the company's.

5 · By procedure

Runbooks, teach-backs, after-action reviews, and a training calendar. The way an artillery battery, a plebe year, and a track season are run.

Talent does not scale; procedure does. Every recurring thing in the program has a written standard, an owner, and a check. A tutor is certified by teaching a lesson back against the playbook, not by résumé. Every cohort ends with an after-action review: what was supposed to happen, what happened, why, and what changes in the playbook. The playbook is versioned.

The student's plan is periodized like an athlete's season: a base phase for fluency and sentences, a build phase for the harder reasoning units, a peak phase on full timed sections and unfamiliar texts, and a taper in the last week. Enter a test date and the plan lays itself out. The ten principles behind it, from gun crews to track seasons, are on the Training page.

The student's after-action review

Twenty minutes every Sunday. Four questions, in order.

What was supposed to happen

This week's pattern and the plan's phase. Three workspace sessions, one checkpoint, the long read minutes.

What happened

From the log, not memory: independent score, hints per task, the certain-and-wrong list, oral reading numbers, minutes read.

Why

For each gap, one sentence naming the cause: the build of the distractor, the chapter it belongs to, the day the session was skipped.

What changes

One change for next week, written down. Not five. Teams get good by running the standard, not by redesigning it weekly.

Sample

From chapter 2, "Small words, large effects."

Here is a sentence from a school board report. Read it once, the way you would on a test.

The district found that nearly every school that adopted the later start saw attendance improve, although in most cases the improvement could not be separated from other changes made the same year.

Most students come away with "later start, better attendance." That is what the first half says, and the first half is where attention goes. But the sentence has three small words doing most of the work. Nearly tells you some schools did not improve. Although tells you the second half is about to take something back. Most tells you that in the typical case, the district itself could not credit the later start.

So the sentence does not say the later start improved attendance. It says attendance improved, and that nobody knows why. A test question about this sentence will offer you "the later start improved attendance" as a choice, because it is what a fast reader believes the sentence says. The right answer will be the one that sounds weaker.

The habit is simple and it takes about two seconds: before you decide what a sentence says, find the small word and ask what it takes away.

The drill on the facing page

Thirty sentences like the one above. For each, the student circles the qualifier, writes what it removes, and rewrites the sentence without it to feel the difference. The workspace's sentence unpacker does the same thing on screen.

Why the method fits this test

The GMAT removed sentence correction. What is left is reading and reasoning.

The GMAT Focus verbal section is 23 questions in 45 minutes, all reading comprehension and critical reasoning. The GRE's verbal sections are 12 and 15 questions of the same skills with harder vocabulary. Neither test rewards grammar drills anymore. Both reward one habit: find the sentence, ask how far it goes, and know whose claim it is.

Non-native readers lose questions in four places, and they are the same four places our high-school students lose them: qualifiers (some, most, nearly), reasoning verbs (suggests, concedes, counters), whose view a sentence reports, and stamina. The fifth is an idiom and vocabulary tax, which the roots and near-neighbor work in the word banks addresses directly.

Fluency track · automaticity is the foundation

If decoding costs attention, nothing is left for meaning. We measure it weekly and build it on purpose.

Comprehension work only pays off once word recognition is automatic. This track measures oral reading every week (speed, accuracy, phrasing) and drills the three things that move it: repeated reading, phrase-cued reading, and multisyllabic word attack. Checkpoint passages are never used in lessons.

Program benchmarks on grade-level nonfiction

Our bars for the readers we serve, not national norms. A tutor confirms them live at intake and every week after.

2 · Repeated reading

The same paragraph, three times, timed

The highest-yield fluency drill. The third read is usually 20 to 30% faster, and the speed transfers to new text. Pick a paragraph from a lesson you have done.

3 · Phrase-cued reading

Read in chunks, not words

Prosody is comprehension made audible. Marks show where a fluent reader pauses. Read it aloud with the marks, then turn them off and read it again the same way.

4 · Echo reading

Hear a sentence, then say it

Assisted reading for stamina and phrasing. One sentence is read aloud and highlighted; you read it back the same way; then the next.

5 · Multisyllabic word attack

Long academic words, chunk by chunk

Older readers stall on long words, not short ones. Say each word by its chunks, stress the marked syllable, then say it whole three times. Press ♪ to hear it. Click a word when it is automatic.
